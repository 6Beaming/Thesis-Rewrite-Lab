Urban Resilience and Distributed Infrastructure Planning

Working draft, Chapter 2: Literature Review

Selected sentence: high nominalization, passive construction, and weak transition signal.

Recent scholarship has increasingly positioned urban resilience as a multidimensional planning concern rather than a narrow recovery metric. This shift reflects the integration of ecological stability, infrastructure redundancy, and social adaptation within municipal decision-making. The implementation of resilience-oriented policies is often complicated by the fragmentation of governance responsibilities across public agencies and private infrastructure operators.

Although several authors suggest that distributed systems may improve service continuity after climate shocks, the evidence remains uneven across regional contexts. In coastal cities, redundancy in transit, energy, and communication systems can protect vulnerable populations when primary networks fail. However, these benefits are frequently evaluated through technical performance indicators while social accessibility is treated as a secondary concern.

A more useful planning model therefore requires the coordination of linguistic, institutional, and technical assumptions in policy documents. For this reason, this review compares resilience definitions, implementation barriers, and evaluation standards across twenty-four peer-reviewed studies.
